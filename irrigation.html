Why irrigation comes first

Human-driven processes in the water cycle

People manage water at almost every step of the cycle: they pump aquifers, run reservoirs, divert rivers, drain fields and supply cities. Each process follows human decisions, which large-scale water-cycle models still represent poorly.

People manage water in many ways.Irrigation is linked to almost all of them.

  • IrrigationThe largest human intervention in the water cycle.
  • Groundwater pumping and artificial rechargeDirect linkWells pump aquifers to irrigate fields; water that seeps past the roots recharges them.
  • Reservoir operationDirect linkReservoirs store water and release it downstream when crops need it.
  • Surface water abstractionDirect linkPumps draw water from rivers and lakes straight onto fields.
  • Water diversionDirect linkCanals divert river water to irrigation districts, leaving less in the river.
  • Tile drainageDirect linkBuried pipes carry excess water from irrigated fields to ditches and streams.
  • Return flowDirect linkWater the crops don’t use flows back to rivers and aquifers.
  • Water qualityDirect linkRunoff from irrigated fields carries sediment, and return flows change stream temperature.
  • Land management and dynamic vegetationLess direct linkIrrigation changes what is grown and how green the land stays through the season.
  • Urban water managementNot linked hereDraws on the same rivers and aquifers, but is managed separately: outside this first step.

That makes irrigation a strong place to start: modeling it well captures many of the broader ways people interact with the water system.

Why it matters

Why irrigation matters

Feeding a growing world depends on irrigated farms, and irrigated farms depend on fresh water that is already scarce.

Food

×2 food demand by 2050, about 30% from population growth and 70% from diets richer in meat. Foley, 2015

2.3 bn people faced moderate or severe food insecurity in 2021. FAO, 2022

Yield

2× the harvest: irrigated land is at least twice as productive as the same area of rainfed land. World Bank

42% of the world’s irrigated area is in China and India.

Water

70% of fresh water withdrawn worldwide goes to irrigation, over 90% in parts of the Middle East and Southeast Asia. Foley et al., 2011 Map: WRI Aqueduct

40% of irrigated land, and 60% in the US, relies on groundwater, and groundwater depletion roughly doubled from 1960 to 2000. Wada et al., 2010

Climate

70% of the land irrigated in 1995 will need more irrigation water by the 2070s. Döll & Siebert, 2002

41% of irrigation water is taken from rivers’ environmental flows, at a cost to ecosystems. Jägermeyr et al., 2017

Irrigation also changes the climate around it: rain, locally and downwindcooler hot daysa later monsoonhumid heat beyond human tolerance McDermid et al., 2023

The gap

No one knows how much water each farm uses

1Hard to measure

  • Meters are rare. Fitting and reading them is costly and slow; only 36% of US irrigation wells have one. Foster et al., 2020
  • Unrecorded withdrawals. Illegal pumping from rivers and aquifers never shows up in the records.
  • Farmers resist meters, fearing they will lead to regulation.

2Hard to model

  • Fields change. Crop rotation changes what is planted from year to year.
  • Farmers decide, not crops. Irrigation follows calendars, leaf colour, curling and wilting, and the neighbours, not the crop’s water demand.
  • Models give the need, not the use. Most estimate the water crops require, not what farmers apply.

Satellites revisit every field every few days. My work asks how much of the irrigation signal they can recover, and at what scale. It started with a shortcut: irrigation changes the soil moisture, so soil moisture can stand in for it.
