Measuring farm irrigation from space

Irrigation is the largest use of fresh water on Earth, yet almost no one measures it directly. I combine satellite soil moisture and thermal observations with land surface models and data assimilation to detect where, when and how much farmers irrigate.

Why irrigation matters

Feeding a growing population depends on irrigated farms, and irrigated farms depend on water that is already running short.

70%

of the fresh water withdrawn worldwide goes to irrigation, and more than 90% in parts of the Middle East and Southeast Asia.Foley et al., 2011

2×

Irrigated land is at least twice as productive per hectare as rainfed land.World Bank, Water in agriculture

2050

is when global food demand is projected to have roughly doubled, driven more by diets shifting toward meat than by population growth.Tilman & Clark, 2015

41%

of irrigation water use comes at the expense of rivers’ environmental flow requirements.Jägermeyr et al., 2017

How irrigation changes the climate system
  • It can shift evapotranspiration from moisture-limited to energy-limited, changing local water and energy budgets (Taylor et al., 2012).
  • Groundwater-fed irrigation has increased downwind precipitation and streamflow by up to 30% in the High Plains and California’s Central Valley (DeAngelis et al., 2010; Kustu et al., 2011).
  • Large-scale irrigation can also suppress local precipitation (Szilagyi et al., 2020).
  • It cools the hottest days of the year (Thiery et al., 2020; Lawston et al., 2020).
  • It can delay the onset of monsoons (Tuinenburg, 2013).
  • It can create humid heat waves beyond what people can tolerate (Raymond et al., 2020).

Adapted from McDermid et al., 2023, Irrigation in the Earth system.

The measurement gap

To manage water withdrawals, someone first has to know how much water each farm is taking. On the ground, that is surprisingly hard.

Few withdrawals are metered

  • Metering is expensive and labor-intensive.
  • Some withdrawals from rivers and aquifers go unreported or are illegal.
  • Farmers may oppose meters over concerns about future regulation (Foster et al., 2020).

Models estimate need, not decisions

  • Crops rotate from season to season.
  • Farmers often irrigate by rule of thumb, such as leaf color, curling or leaf temperature, rather than modeled crop water demand.
  • Most models estimate the irrigation a crop requires, not what was actually applied.

Satellites revisit every field every few days. My work asks how much of the irrigation signal they can recover, and at what scale.
